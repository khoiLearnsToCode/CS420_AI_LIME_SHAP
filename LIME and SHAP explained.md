# Opening the black box with LIME and SHAP

Two papers changed how we ask an AI model "why?". Ribeiro, Singh and Guestrin's KDD 2016 paper introduced **LIME** (Local Interpretable Model-agnostic Explanations). LIME explains one prediction of any model by making many small changes to the input, watching how the model reacts, and fitting a simple straight-line model to those reactions in the neighbourhood of that one input ([Ribeiro et al., ar5iv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1602.04938)). A year later, Lundberg and Lee's NeurIPS 2017 paper introduced **SHAP** (SHapley Additive exPlanations). SHAP showed that LIME and five other popular methods all produce the same kind of explanation: a starting value plus one "push" per feature. It then proved that only one way of computing those pushes meets three common-sense fairness rules, and that way is the **Shapley value** from game theory ([Lundberg & Lee, NeurIPS paper](https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper_files/paper/2017/file/8a20a8621978632d76c43dfd28b67767-Paper.pdf)). The link between the two papers is very direct: **Kernel SHAP is LIME's own recipe with its settings chosen by theory instead of by hand**, and with those settings LIME's answer becomes the Shapley value. Both methods catch models that are "right for the wrong reasons." In LIME's best-known demonstration, showing explanations dropped trust in a model that was secretly a snow detector from 10 of 27 ML-trained students to 3 of 27 ([Ribeiro et al., arXiv v2](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1602.04938v2)). They are also approximations with known weak points. They can be unstable, they get confused when features are correlated, they can be deliberately fooled, and they never by themselves tell you what *causes* what. This report teaches both ideas from the ground up, the way I would in a first lecture: intuition first, then small numbers you can check by hand, then an honest account of the limits.

## A 94%-accurate model can be completely wrong, and accuracy alone won't show it

Start with the problem both papers try to solve. Modern models such as deep neural networks or forests of hundreds of decision trees are often **black boxes**: you put an input in, a prediction comes out, and even experts cannot easily say why. Lundberg and Lee describe this as a real tension, because the most accurate models on large datasets tend to be the hardest to interpret ([Lundberg & Lee, abstract](https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper_files/paper/2017/hash/8a20a8621978632d76c43dfd28b67767-Abstract.html)). They give three reasons to want explanations anyway: to decide whether to trust a model, to improve it, and to learn something about the process being modelled ([Lundberg & Lee](https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper_files/paper/2017/file/8a20a8621978632d76c43dfd28b67767-Paper.pdf)).

Ribeiro and colleagues split "trust" into two questions that are worth keeping separate. **Trusting a prediction** means deciding whether to act on a single answer, such as a doctor deciding whether to accept "this patient has the flu." **Trusting a model** means deciding whether the whole system will behave sensibly once deployed ([Ribeiro et al., arXiv v2](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1602.04938v2)). The usual tool for the second question is to test accuracy on held-out data. The paper argues this can mislead badly, for two reasons. The first is **data leakage**, where a clue sneaks into the data that won't exist in real life; their example is a patient ID number that happens to correlate with the diagnosis. The second is **dataset shift**, where the real world looks different from the training data ([Ribeiro et al., ar5iv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1602.04938)).

Their text example makes this concrete. A classifier trained to tell "Christianity" posts from "Atheism" posts in a well-known newsgroup dataset scored **94% accuracy** on held-out data. When LIME explained its predictions, the important words had nothing to do with religion. They were email-header leftovers like "Posting," "Host," and "Re," plus the names of frequent posters. The word "Posting" appeared in **22% of training documents, and 99% of those were labelled Atheism** ([Ribeiro et al., ar5iv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1602.04938)). The model had learned a quirk of how the data was collected, not anything about religion.

The image example is the one students remember. The authors **deliberately** trained a bad husky-versus-wolf classifier on 20 hand-picked photos in which every wolf stood in snow and no husky did. The model quietly learned "snow means wolf." They showed 27 graduate students who had taken at least one graduate machine-learning course ten of its predictions. Eight were correct. Two were wrong: a husky in snow called "wolf," and a wolf on grass called "husky." Before seeing explanations, **10 of 27 said they trusted the model** and only 12 mentioned snow or background as a possible clue. After seeing LIME's explanations, which highlighted the snowy patches of each photo, **only 3 of 27 still trusted it and 25 of 27 named snow** ([Ribeiro et al., arXiv v2](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1602.04938v2)).

The lesson is that a high score tells you *that* a model is often right, not *why*. An explanation is a way to look over the model's shoulder.

> **Check your understanding.** The wolf model got 8 of 10 test pictures right. Why would a careful engineer still refuse to deploy it?
>
> *Answer:* Because it gets them right for the wrong reason. In the wild, a husky in snow or a wolf on a summer hillside will fool it. Accuracy on a test set that shares the same quirk (wolves always in snow) cannot reveal this.

## LIME zooms in until the curve looks straight

### The core intuition

Picture the model's decision as a wiggly border on a map, with "wolf" on one side and "husky" on the other. Globally, that border can be very curvy. But zoom in far enough on any single point, the way the ground under your feet looks flat even though the Earth is round, and the border looks almost like a straight line. A straight line is easy to read: its tilt in each direction tells you which way each feature pushes. That is all LIME does. It zooms in on *one* input and draws a straight-line approximation that is faithful *nearby*, even though it would be wrong far away. The paper's own diagram shows exactly this: a curvy blue/pink background (the black box), a bold red cross (the input being explained), sample points sized by how close they are, and a dashed line that is right locally and wrong globally ([Ribeiro et al., ar5iv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1602.04938)). An online course uses a related picture: standing in a mountain range, you can tell which way the ground slopes where you are, but that says nothing about the whole range ([APXML course](https://apxml.com/courses/model-interpretability-explainability/chapter-2-lime-local-interpretability/lime-intuition)).

The authors list four qualities they want in an explainer. It should be **interpretable** (a human can read it, so it shouldn't list hundreds of features). It should be **locally faithful** (it matches the model near this input). It should be **model-agnostic** (it works on any model by only asking it questions, never opening it up). And it should offer a **global perspective** (a way to judge the whole model, which comes later via SP-LIME) ([Ribeiro et al., ar5iv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1602.04938)).

### The recipe, step by step

The key move comes first: describe the input in **human-sized pieces** that can be switched on and off. For text, the pieces are words, and each word is either present or deleted. For images, they are **superpixels**, small patches of similar-looking pixels, and each patch is either shown or greyed out. This on/off description is a list of 1s and 0s, which the paper writes as x′ (read "x-prime"). It need not match how the model itself sees the input. The model might see pixel values or word embeddings, but the *explanation* talks about words and patches ([Ribeiro et al., ar5iv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1602.04938)).

LIME then repeats the following loop thousands of times. **Step 1:** make a perturbed copy by randomly switching off some of the pieces (some words deleted, some patches greyed). **Step 2:** feed the copy to the black box and record its prediction. **Step 3:** give that copy a weight based on how similar it is to the original, so near-copies count a lot and heavily damaged copies count little. **Step 4:** after collecting all the samples, fit a small weighted linear model that predicts the black box's output from the on/off switches, keeping at most K pieces (for example the 10 most important words). The coefficients of that little model are the explanation ([Ribeiro et al., ar5iv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1602.04938)).

The paper packages this as a balancing act, written ξ(x) = argmin over g of L(f, g, π_x) + Ω(g). Every symbol has a plain meaning:

| Symbol | What it means in plain words |
|---|---|
| f | the black-box model; f(x) is its predicted probability for a class |
| g | the simple "explanation model" we are trying to find (here, a straight-line formula) |
| π_x | the closeness weight: how much a perturbed copy resembles the original x |
| L(f, g, π_x) | how badly g disagrees with f on the weighted samples (lower means more faithful) |
| Ω(g) | how complicated g is (for example, how many words it uses) |
| argmin | "pick the g that makes the total smallest" |

So LIME looks for the simplest straight-line story that still matches the model closely near this one input ([Ribeiro et al., ar5iv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1602.04938)). In the concrete version, the closeness weight is an exponential "kernel" that shrinks with distance, using cosine distance for text and ordinary straight-line distance for images. A width setting σ (sigma) controls how fast the weight falls off, and the K most useful features are picked with a method the authors call K-LASSO ([Ribeiro et al., ar5iv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1602.04938)). Keep σ in mind. It decides how far you zoom in, and it will come back in the section on limitations.

### A worked example you can do by hand

*Illustrative.* Suppose a spam filter reads the email "**free** prize, you are a **winner**," and we explain it using just two words. LIME deletes words in every combination and asks the model each time:

| "free" present? | "winner" present? | Model's spam probability |
|---|---|---|
| no | no | 0.10 |
| yes | no | 0.70 |
| no | yes | 0.40 |
| yes | yes | 0.90 (the real email) |

Say the closeness kernel gives the untouched email weight 1, each one-word-deleted version weight 0.5, and the version with both words deleted weight 0.1. Fitting a weighted straight line, spam ≈ base + w_free·(free) + w_winner·(winner), gives roughly **base 0.17, free +0.52, winner +0.22**. Read the result like this: near this email, "free" pushes spam probability up about twice as much as "winner." That is a useful, readable explanation. Notice one small flaw: 0.17 + 0.52 + 0.22 = 0.91, not exactly the model's 0.90. LIME's line is a good approximation, but nothing forces it to add up. Change the kernel weights and the numbers move slightly too; with equal weights you would get 0.13, 0.55 and 0.25. SHAP will fix both of these points.

The paper also shows LIME on a real image network, Google's Inception. A photo of a man with a guitar and a dog was labelled "electric guitar" (p = 0.32), "acoustic guitar" (0.24) and "Labrador" (0.21). LIME's explanation for "electric guitar" highlighted the fretboard, and for "Labrador" it highlighted the dog. The top answer was wrong, but the explanation shows the mistake was a *reasonable* one, which is itself a form of trust ([Ribeiro et al., ar5iv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1602.04938)). Speed depends on the model. Explaining a 1,000-tree random forest with 5,000 samples took under 3 seconds on a laptop, while one Inception image took about 10 minutes, almost all of it spent waiting on the network ([Ribeiro et al., ar5iv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1602.04938)).

### From one prediction to a whole model: SP-LIME

One explanation answers "should I trust this prediction?", but deciding whether to trust a *model* needs a broader view, and people only have time to read a handful of examples. **SP-LIME** ("submodular pick") chooses a small budget of B examples whose explanations, taken together, cover as many of the important features as possible without repeating each other. Think of a highlight reel: you want every major reason the model uses to show up at least once, not the same reason six times. Each extra example adds less new information than the one before it (mathematicians call this diminishing-returns property "submodular"). Because of that, simply choosing the most useful next example each time is guaranteed to get at least about **63% (1 − 1/e)** of the best possible coverage ([Ribeiro et al., ar5iv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1602.04938)).

> **Check your understanding.** If you set the closeness width σ very large, so that heavily damaged copies count almost as much as the original, what happens to the "zoom"?
>
> *Answer:* You zoom *out*. The straight line now tries to fit the model over a wide region where the boundary is curvy, so it becomes less faithful to this particular input.

## Experiments show explanations help even non-experts pick better models

LIME's evaluation used both simulated users and real people, and its strongest results are about human decisions.

In **simulated tests**, the authors explained models that are transparent by design, such as sparse logistic regression and small decision trees, so they knew the true important features. LIME recovered **over 90%** of them on book and DVD review data (for example, 92.1% and 97.0% on books) ([Ribeiro et al., arXiv v2](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1602.04938v2)). They also randomly labelled a quarter of the features "untrustworthy" and asked whether a simulated user reading the explanations could tell which predictions depended on them. LIME scored F1 values around **92–97**, ahead of the comparison methods ("parzen" at roughly 82–94, "greedy" around 45–58, and random around 14–15) ([Ribeiro et al., arXiv v2](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1602.04938v2)). F1 is a 0–100 score that balances catching problems against raising false alarms. These baselines are 2016-era methods. The notes describing exactly how "parzen" and "greedy" work came from background knowledge and were not verified against the paper, so I won't characterize them further here.

The **human studies** are where it gets convincing. The authors built two religion classifiers. The *original* one scored 94.0% on the standard test set but only **57.3%** on a fresh collection of 819-per-class religion web pages it had never seen. A *cleaned* version, with header words and poster names removed, scored lower on the standard test (88.6%) but **69.0%** on the fresh pages. Judged on test accuracy alone, you would pick the worse model. Mechanical Turk workers, mostly not ML experts, saw six documents with six-word explanations from each model and were asked which would work better in the real world. With LIME plus SP-LIME's chosen examples, **89% picked correctly**, compared with 75% for LIME on random examples and 68% for the weakest baseline combination ([Ribeiro et al., arXiv v2](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1602.04938v2)).

In a second study, non-experts **improved** a bad classifier by crossing out words in the explanations that they thought wouldn't generalize, after which the model was retrained without them. Over three rounds, averaging **about 3.6 minutes per round**, accuracy on the unseen religion pages went up and varied less between users. The paper's figure shows the trend, but the notes did not capture a single final accuracy number, so I won't quote one ([Ribeiro et al., arXiv v2](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1602.04938v2)). The husky/wolf study described earlier completes the human evidence.

> **Check your understanding.** In the classifier-choice study, why could explanations beat accuracy?
>
> *Answer:* The test set shared the training data's quirks (email headers), so accuracy rewarded the model that exploited them. Explanations exposed *which words* each model relied on, and people could see that "Posting" and "Host" have nothing to do with religion.

## Shapley values split the credit fairly, and SHAP proves only one split is fair

### Every explainer writes the same kind of receipt

Lundberg and Lee's first insight is to treat an explanation as a small model in its own right, which they call the **explanation model** g. Many methods, it turns out, produce the same shape of explanation, which the paper calls an **additive feature attribution**:

g(z′) = φ₀ + φ₁z′₁ + φ₂z′₂ + … + φ_M z′_M

Here is what each symbol means. **z′** is the same on/off list as in LIME (1 = feature present, 0 = absent). **M** is the number of features. **φ₀** ("phi-zero") is the starting value. Each **φᵢ** is the push, up or down, credited to feature i ([Lundberg & Lee](https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper_files/paper/2017/file/8a20a8621978632d76c43dfd28b67767-Paper.pdf)). It helps to read it as a receipt: a base amount, then one line item per feature, totalling the prediction. The paper shows that **six existing methods**, namely LIME, DeepLIFT, layer-wise relevance propagation, Shapley regression values, Shapley sampling values, and Quantitative Input Influence, all produce receipts of this shape. They differ only in how they compute the line items ([Lundberg & Lee](https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper_files/paper/2017/file/8a20a8621978632d76c43dfd28b67767-Paper.pdf)). That observation turns a crowded toolbox into one question: which way of filling in the receipt is right?

### Three common-sense rules, one answer

The paper proposes three properties a good receipt should have ([Lundberg & Lee](https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper_files/paper/2017/file/8a20a8621978632d76c43dfd28b67767-Paper.pdf)). **Local accuracy:** the line items must add up exactly to the model's actual prediction for this input. **Missingness:** a feature that isn't present gets zero credit. **Consistency:** if you change the model so that a feature matters more (or equally) in every situation, that feature's credit must not go down. **Theorem 1** then proves that exactly one way of filling in the receipt satisfies all three: the **Shapley value**, a fair-division rule from cooperative game theory. As a direct consequence, any method not based on Shapley values breaks local accuracy, consistency, or both ([Lundberg & Lee](https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper_files/paper/2017/file/8a20a8621978632d76c43dfd28b67767-Paper.pdf)). The SHAP documentation sums up the framing this way: features are the "players," the prediction is the "payout," and Shapley values divide the payout fairly ([SHAP docs](https://shap-community.readthedocs.io/en/latest/)).

### The taxi-fare analogy

Shapley values are easiest to understand with money. Three friends share a taxi from Luton airport to Cambridge, London and Oxford at £3 a mile, for a total fare of **£483**. How should they split it? Charging the first person dropped off the least seems unfair, because it depends on an arbitrary order. The Shapley answer is to imagine *every possible pickup order*, work out how much extra each rider adds to the fare in each order, and average. In this worked example, the costs if only some riders went are: Cambridge alone £114, London alone £102, Oxford alone £150, Cambridge+London £285, Cambridge+Oxford £381, London+Oxford £288. Averaging over all six orders gives **Cambridge £172, London £119.50, Oxford £191.50**, which sums to exactly £483 ([Towards Data Science](https://towardsdatascience.com/shapley-values-clearly-explained-a7f7ef22b104/)). London pays least because adding a London stop barely lengthens the trip. The same idea is often told as a team splitting a bonus by each member's average added value. That version is common in teaching, but the notes found no primary source for it, so treat it as folklore.

The paper's formula is just this "average over all orders" written compactly. Its factorial weights, |z′|!(M − |z′| − 1)!/M!, are simply the fraction of orderings in which a particular group of features arrived before feature i ([Lundberg & Lee](https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper_files/paper/2017/file/8a20a8621978632d76c43dfd28b67767-Paper.pdf)). You don't need to memorize it. "Average the extra contribution over every arrival order" is the whole idea.

### Back to the spam email, now with Shapley

*Illustrative.* Use the same four model outputs as in the LIME example. With no words, the score is 0.10. That is our starting value φ₀.

| Arrival order | "free" adds | "winner" adds |
|---|---|---|
| free first, then winner | 0.70 − 0.10 = **+0.60** | 0.90 − 0.70 = **+0.20** |
| winner first, then free | 0.90 − 0.40 = **+0.50** | 0.40 − 0.10 = **+0.30** |
| **Average (Shapley value)** | **+0.55** | **+0.25** |

Check the receipt: 0.10 + 0.55 + 0.25 = **0.90**, exactly the model's prediction. Local accuracy holds by construction. Compare LIME's earlier answer (0.17 + 0.52 + 0.22 = 0.91). It had the same story and the same ranking, but it didn't quite balance, and it depended on the kernel weights we happened to choose.

A second toy example shows how SHAP handles features that **boost each other**. *Illustrative:* a house-price model predicts $200k on average, $250k if we know only that the house is large, $230k if we know only that the location is good, and $300k for this large house in a good location. Size-then-location credits size +50 and location +50. Location-then-size credits location +30 and size +70. Averaging gives **size $60k, location $40k**, and 200 + 60 + 40 = 300. The extra $20k that comes from the two features *together* (100 is more than 50 + 30) gets split evenly between them. This also shows a quiet limitation: a single number per feature has to fold interaction effects into the individual credits.

The paper ran a small human study on exactly this sort of question. In one scenario, a "sickness score" is 5 if a patient has exactly one of fever or cough, but only 2 if they have both, and 0 if neither. Working it through by orderings gives each symptom +1, which totals 2. In another, three people answered 5, 4 and 0 quiz questions correctly and the profit is the *maximum*, $5. Shapley gives **$3, $2 and $0**, rather than all of it to the top scorer or an equal split. The paper reports that Mechanical Turk participants' most common answers **agreed much more strongly with SHAP** than with LIME or DeepLIFT ([Lundberg & Lee](https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper_files/paper/2017/file/8a20a8621978632d76c43dfd28b67767-Paper.pdf)). One caution: the exact dollar amounts the human participants gave were not captured in the research notes, so I can't confirm they matched $3/$2/$0 precisely. The $3/$2/$0 split is my own calculation of the Shapley values.

### What "missing" means, and the SHAP value precisely

To ask "what would the model say without this feature?", you need a rule for removing a feature. SHAP defines it as **averaging the model's prediction over the possible values of the missing features**, in technical terms the conditional expectation E[f(z) | z_S], where S is the set of features we know. The starting value φ₀ is then the model's **average prediction over the data**, which is what you would guess knowing nothing about this input ([Lundberg & Lee](https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper_files/paper/2017/file/8a20a8621978632d76c43dfd28b67767-Paper.pdf)). So a SHAP explanation always reads: "Start from the average prediction. Feature A pushed it up this much, feature B pushed it down that much…, and the pushes add up exactly to this prediction." Because the exact version is expensive, the paper's practical estimators make simplifying assumptions. They often assume **features are independent** of each other, and sometimes also that the model is **linear**, in which case missing features are just replaced by their averages ([Lundberg & Lee](https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper_files/paper/2017/file/8a20a8621978632d76c43dfd28b67767-Paper.pdf)). Hold on to that independence assumption. It matters later.

> **Check your understanding.** A SHAP explanation for a loan model says: base value 0.30, income +0.25, debt −0.10, age +0.05. What did the model actually predict for this applicant?
>
> *Answer:* 0.30 + 0.25 − 0.10 + 0.05 = **0.50**. Local accuracy guarantees the receipt totals the real prediction.

## Kernel SHAP is LIME with the knobs set by theory

This is the unifying result. LIME had three hand-chosen "knobs": the closeness kernel π, the error measure L, and the complexity penalty Ω. The SHAP paper points out that because LIME picks these by rule of thumb, its answers **do not in general equal Shapley values** and can break local accuracy or consistency ([Lundberg & Lee](https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper_files/paper/2017/file/8a20a8621978632d76c43dfd28b67767-Paper.pdf)). **Theorem 2** shows that if you set the knobs to specific values, LIME's weighted regression returns exactly the Shapley values. The settings are: **no complexity penalty** (Ω = 0); squared error for L, as LIME already used; and a particular weight called the **Shapley kernel**, π(z′) = (M − 1) / [C(M, |z′|) · |z′| · (M − |z′|)] ([Lundberg & Lee](https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper_files/paper/2017/file/8a20a8621978632d76c43dfd28b67767-Paper.pdf)).

The symbols are less scary than they look. **|z′|** is how many features are switched on in a sample, **M** is the total number of features, and **C(M, |z′|)** counts how many ways there are to choose that many features. What the formula *does* is the important part. It gives the **most weight to samples where almost everything is off or almost everything is on**, and infinite weight to the two extremes, all-off and all-on. Those infinite weights force the receipt to start at the baseline and end at the actual prediction. This is the opposite of LIME's intuition of "near copies matter most," and the paper's figure shows the Shapley kernel's U shape against earlier heuristic kernels ([Lundberg & Lee](https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper_files/paper/2017/file/8a20a8621978632d76c43dfd28b67767-Paper.pdf)). Why the extremes? A sample with only one feature on tells you that feature's effect alone, and a sample with only one feature off tells you its effect given everything else. Both are very informative about individual credit.

You can watch this work in the spam example. With M = 2, the two one-word samples each get weight 1/2. The empty and full samples get infinite weight, which pins the base at 0.10 and the total at 0.90. The regression must then split 0.80 between the two words while matching the one-word samples (0.60 and 0.30 above base) as closely as possible. The best split is **0.55 and 0.25**, the Shapley values we found by averaging orderings. Same data, same regression machinery as LIME, different weights, and now the receipt balances.

The paper adds model-specific shortcuts. **Linear SHAP** reads SHAP values straight off a linear model: each feature's credit is its weight times how far its value is from average, φᵢ = wᵢ(xᵢ − E[xᵢ]) (assuming independent features). **Deep SHAP** adapts DeepLIFT, a fast method for neural networks, so that it approximates SHAP values ([Lundberg & Lee](https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper_files/paper/2017/file/8a20a8621978632d76c43dfd28b67767-Paper.pdf)). The paper's experiments are small demonstrations rather than large benchmarks. On two synthetic decision trees, Kernel SHAP reached accurate estimates with **fewer model calls** than classic Shapley sampling, while LIME's estimates differed noticeably from the true Shapley values. On a digit-recognition network asked "why is this an 8 and not a 3?", hiding the pixels picked by SHAP and the improved DeepLIFT appears from the paper's plot to shift the prediction more than hiding those picked by LIME or the original DeepLIFT. The text gives no exact numbers for this, so treat it as a visual reading ([Lundberg & Lee](https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper_files/paper/2017/file/8a20a8621978632d76c43dfd28b67767-Paper.pdf)).

A note on what came later: the fast, exact **Tree SHAP** algorithm for tree ensembles is *not* in the 2017 paper. It came in follow-up work by Lundberg and colleagues, which computes exact Shapley values for trees in polynomial time and combines many local explanations into global summary plots, with medical case studies such as a mortality dataset of 14,407 people ([Lundberg et al., arXiv 1905.04610](https://arxiv.org/abs/1905.04610)). That paper is often cited as appearing in *Nature Machine Intelligence*, but the venue was not confirmed in the research notes.

### LIME and SHAP side by side

| | LIME (2016) | SHAP (2017) |
|---|---|---|
| Core question | What simple line matches the model *near this input*? | How should the prediction be *fairly split* among features? |
| How it computes | Random perturbations, closeness weights, sparse weighted linear fit | Shapley values; Kernel SHAP uses the same regression with the Shapley kernel and no penalty |
| Guarantees | None beyond "approximately faithful nearby" | Local accuracy, missingness, consistency (unique solution, Theorem 1) |
| Do the pushes add up to the prediction? | Not necessarily | Yes, by design |
| Key settings to choose | Kernel width σ, number of features K, how pieces are defined | Background data defining "missing"; independence assumption |
| Local vs global | Local; SP-LIME picks a representative set | Local, and averages naturally into global summaries |
| Speed | Seconds for tabular/text; slow for big image nets | Model-agnostic version can be costly; fast exact versions exist for trees (later work) |

Researchers comparing the two in medical prediction note that LIME is local only, while SHAP supports both local and global views, and that both remain post hoc tools whose answers depend on the model being explained ([Salih et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2305.02012)). Both are widely used. One comparison of ten common explanation methods found SHAP and LIME had the most GitHub stars ([Salih et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2305.02012)), and the LIME paper alone has over 15,000 citations on one aggregator ([Emergent Mind](https://emergentmind.com/papers/1602.04938)). SHAP's citation count was not retrieved, so I won't state one.

> **Check your understanding.** In one sentence, what is the relationship between LIME and Kernel SHAP?
>
> *Answer:* Kernel SHAP *is* LIME's weighted linear regression, but with no sparsity penalty and a specific theory-derived weighting (the Shapley kernel), which makes its coefficients equal the Shapley values.

## Both methods ask "what if?" about inputs the model never saw, and that is their weak spot

Every limitation below comes from one design choice. To explain, both methods feed the model **artificial inputs**, such as emails with words deleted, photos with patches greyed out, or rows with features swapped for averages, and treat its answers as evidence. Keep that in mind and the pitfalls stop being surprising.

**Instability.** LIME draws a fresh random sample every time, so running it twice on the same input can give different explanations. One study on a credit-scoring model found that with one setting of the kernel width, explanations were stable across repeated calls (about 89–93% agreement). With a narrower width they agreed only **14% of the time on which features matter**, giving what the authors called totally different explanations. In high dimensions, LIME's sense of "nearby" broke down and a 100-feature model produced explanations in which all features looked about equally important ([Visani et al., arXiv 2001.11757](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2001.11757)). The LIME authors themselves admit the representation can miss what the model uses. Their example is a model that calls sepia-toned photos "retro," which no on/off-patch explanation can express. They also note that a straight line can be unfaithful if the model is very curvy even nearby ([Ribeiro et al., ar5iv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1602.04938)).

**Correlated features.** If height and weight move together, "removing" height while keeping weight produces imaginary people, such as someone 2 metres tall weighing 40 kg. Shapley-based calculations that assume independence can generate such unrealistic inputs, and correlated features can receive misleadingly low credit. LIME also samples features independently ([Salih et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2305.02012)). The SHAP authors' own group later argued there are two legitimate ways to remove a feature, and you must choose by goal. The **interventional** way breaks correlations and is "true to the model," useful when asking what this model does. The **observational** way respects correlations and is "true to the data," but it can give credit to features the model never uses ([Chen, Janizek, Lundberg & Lee, arXiv 2006.16234](https://arxiv.org/abs/2006.16234)). Relatedly, different models trained on the same data agreed on their top three SHAP features but ranked the rest differently, and LIME sometimes even flipped a feature's direction between models ([Salih et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2305.02012)).

**They can be fooled on purpose.** Because the perturbed inputs look visibly different from real data, a dishonest model can tell them apart. Slack and colleagues built a "scaffolding" classifier that used race on real inputs but switched to an innocent model whenever it detected an artificial sample. On a criminal-justice dataset, this hid race from the top of LIME's explanations for **100%** of test cases and from SHAP's for **84%**, in one case taking race's LIME weight from −0.42 to 0.0. They concluded that post hoc explanations alone are not enough to detect discrimination ([Slack et al., AIES 2020](https://www.aies-conference.com/2020/wp-content/papers/174.pdf)). SHAP was harder to fool than LIME, but not immune.

**Explanation is not causation.** A SHAP value tells you what the *model* relies on, not what causes the outcome in the world. The SHAP documentation demonstrates this with a simulated subscription business. "Bugs reported" appears to *increase* renewals, because customers who use the product heavily both report bugs and renew. Discounts appear to *hurt* renewals, because they go to customers who were already wavering. Ad spend looks helpful even though its true effect is zero. Answering causal questions needs different tools, such as causal diagrams, specialised causal estimation, or experiments ([SHAP docs](https://shap.readthedocs.io/en/latest/example_notebooks/overviews/Be%20careful%20when%20interpreting%20predictive%20models%20in%20search%20of%20causal%20insights.html)). Molnar's list of interpretation pitfalls makes the same points, adding warnings about ignoring uncertainty and about high computational cost in many dimensions ([Molnar, Mindful Modeler](https://mindfulmodeler.substack.com/p/8-pitfalls-to-avoid-when-interpreting)).

**Cost and assumptions.** The SHAP paper openly says exact computation is challenging. Its practical estimators lean on the independence assumption and sometimes linearity, and the authors call for faster methods that make fewer assumptions ([Lundberg & Lee](https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper_files/paper/2017/file/8a20a8621978632d76c43dfd28b67767-Paper.pdf)). The Shapley theorem guarantees fairness *given* a definition of "missing." It does not guarantee that the definition fits your question.

These tools also show up in debates about accountability. The EU AI Act's Article 86 gives people affected by certain high-risk AI decisions a right to "clear and meaningful explanations" ([AlgoSoc](https://algosoc.org/results/on-the-relative-importance-of-the-ai-act-right-to-explanation)), and in 2026 the obligations for stand-alone high-risk systems were postponed to December 2027 ([Gibson Dunn](https://www.gibsondunn.com/eu-ai-act-omnibus-agreement-postponed-high-risk-deadlines-and-other-key-changes/)). Whether Article 86 itself moves with that delay was not verified. No law reviewed names LIME or SHAP. They are one possible way to meet such duties, not a required one.

> **Check your understanding.** A SHAP chart shows "number of firefighters sent" strongly increasing predicted fire damage. Should the city send fewer firefighters?
>
> *Answer:* No. Big fires cause more firefighters to be sent *and* more damage. The model learned a correlation, and SHAP faithfully reports what the model uses, not what causes damage.

## Conclusion

The deeper lesson of these two papers is that "explanation" was turned from a vague wish into a precise question with a precise answer, *and* that the precise answer moved the hard choices somewhere else rather than removing them. LIME showed that you can explain any model just by questioning it, and that this lets ordinary people catch a 94%-accurate model cheating. SHAP showed that the many competing explainers were all writing the same kind of receipt, and that only one way of filling it in is fair under three reasonable rules. With the Kernel SHAP theorem it showed that LIME was one setting away from that fair answer all along. What remains contested is the step both methods share: deciding what it means for a feature to be "absent," and trusting the model's answers to inputs it never saw. Instability, correlated-feature artifacts, deliberate fooling and the correlation-versus-cause trap all come from there.

For a near-beginner, a good working rule is to treat a LIME or SHAP explanation like a witness statement rather than a verdict. It tells you what the model appears to be paying attention to, which is enough to catch a snow detector posing as a wolf detector. But you should check it, by rerunning it, by asking how "missing" was defined, by watching for correlated features, and by never reading it as cause and effect, before you stake a decision on it. Used that way, these two papers give you something accuracy scores never could: a way to ask a black box *why*, and to know how far to trust the answer.
